Simulation benchmark · simulated 911 calls · 4 buildings · 7 call types

How much faster does the app find the caller?

We simulated 911 calls from inside four real and synthetic buildings and timed three ways of finding the caller once the crew is at the door: a full sweep from the address alone, an informed dispatcher relaying what the caller said, and the app's ranked search plan. Every comparison uses the same callers, entrance, walking speeds and ambulance arrival time. Only the search order differs.

Read this as a simulation, not field evidence. Callers, phone fixes and room-search times are modelled, and survival comes from published curves applied to the simulated times.

Method

What was simulated

Each simulated call draws a true room from the app's occupancy model, a caller persona matched to the emergency (a panicked bystander, an elderly visitor, an injured caller), a phone fix with realistic error, and the location clues that caller would give, including wrong floors and hedges. Each search method then searches the building from the same entrance until it reaches the true room.

What the saved minutes are worth

The faster the ambulance arrives, the more a minute saved inside matters, because the patient is still on the steep part of the survival curve. In a system with 4-minute ambulance arrival, the gain for a witnessed arrest with bystander CPR is about survival points per call.

Limitations

What this does not show

  • No real calls. Callers, their clues and their mistakes come from the project's own persona model.
  • The caller's location is drawn from the app's own occupancy prior. When it is drawn uniformly instead, the app's median advantage falls to zero and it is slower than the dispatcher on 40% of calls. The prior's room-type and hour-of-day weights have not been checked against real incident locations.
  • One crew searches alone. In a real incident, more units arrive and people in the building direct the crew. That shortens the long tails of every method, the address-only sweep most of all.
  • Room-search times and the 90% detection rate are unverified planning settings, and every look is assumed to find a person who is there. Fire uses a measured hose-advance rate (0.04 m/s) for every room, which makes fire search times very long in absolute terms.
  • Outcome models are coarse. Cardiac arrest uses published logistic curves with an assumed 1.2 minutes from collapse to call. Fire, stroke and internal bleeding use linear per-minute rates, and fire survival is floored at zero. Overdose has no published curve and is reported as time only.
  • The app is slower than the dispatcher on about 1 call in 5, usually when the caller is in a room the evidence makes look unlikely. The averages above include those calls.
Reproduce

Run it yourself

All code is in eval/benchmark/. The main benchmark needs no network. Outputs land in eval/results/.

uv run python -m eval.benchmark.simulate --main 400 --robust 150   # 32,200 calls, about 5 minutes on 20 cores
uv run --with matplotlib --with pandas python -m eval.benchmark.analyze
