ELC — Emergency Location Console · HackPrinceton 2026

Find the 911 caller inside the building.

A phone fix gives an address and a rough height. The caller says things like "I'm locked in a bathroom". We combine both with the building's floor plan to give every room a probability, and hand responders the search order with the shortest expected time to reach the caller.

How it works

Three kinds of evidence, each turned into a likelihood per room and multiplied together. Each new sentence from the caller updates the result.

core/phone_likelihood.py

Phone location

Horizontal fix plus barometric height (the z-axis). Height is compared with each floor, so a 12.6 m reading narrows the call to floors 3 and 4.

parser/agent.py · parser/layout.py

What the caller says

Claude Sonnet 5.5 reads each turn against a map of the building's layout and works out which rooms the words fit: "the last door past the stairs", "a big corner room", "up high, the name starts with a B". No fixed word list. Offline, keyword rules take over.

core/prior.py · configs/priors.yaml

Weighing the prior

Before the caller says anything, each room gets a starting probability from three things: how big it is, what kind of room it is, and the time of day.

Area counts less than proportionally (the exponent is 0.7), so a large open office does not swamp everything else. The time of day shifts weight between room types: at night an office drops to 0.3 of its daytime weight while storage and plant rooms double.

5% is held back for "the caller is somewhere the model does not cover", so the console can say so instead of forcing an answer.

π(r) ∝ area(r)^0.7 × w(type, use, hour) × occupancy(r)

parser/agent.py · parser/layout.py · parser/prompts/reason_call.md

Reading the caller

Callers rarely give a room number. They say what they can see, hear or remember, in fragments spread across the call. The reader is Claude Sonnet 5.5, given a layout map of the building: every hallway with its rooms in door order and what sits at each end, and for each room its size and size rank on the floor, whether it is a corner, on an outside wall or interior, which door it is along which hallway, the walking distance to the stairs, the elevator and the nearest entrance, its windows, its neighbours, and its suite and tenant.

There is no fixed vocabulary. The model reasons from the layout to the rooms the words fit, and it keeps a running picture of the whole call: "up high", then "far from where I came in", then "the name starts with a B" narrows a 208-room building to one suite on the top floor. Besides the location it records facts for the crew (hazards, a door code, who will meet them) and suggests the kind of emergency.

Code checks every record. The quoted words must appear in what the caller actually said, room ids must exist, and nothing is ever written as verified. If the model cannot be reached, the offline keyword rules read the turn instead.

core/fusion.py · core/clue_likelihood.py · core/phone_likelihood.py

Weighing the phone fix and what the caller says

Each piece of evidence scores every room between 0 and 1 for how well it fits, and the scores are multiplied onto the prior (Bayes' rule).

Phone. The barometric height is compared with each floor's height plus 1.2 m for a held phone, with a spread of 2.34 m (±3 m covers 80% of readings). The horizontal fix is scored by distance to the room.

Speech. A clue scores 1 on the rooms the reader says it fits and a small "could still be" value (5–50%) on the rest. A stated floor scores 1 on that floor and 0.25 on the floors next to it, because callers miscount. In the offline keyword reader, "I'm in a bathroom" scores restrooms 1, and a route ("up the stairs, then right") is scored by walking it to each room and comparing the turns.

p(r | E) ∝ π(r) × L_phone(r) × ∏ F_i(r) L_phone(r) = L_horizontal(r) × L_height(r) L_height(r) = exp(−½ ((z − floor_r − 1.2) / 2.34)²)

F_i is the factor for the caller's i-th statement, defined in the next block.

core/fusion.py · configs/clue_vocab.yaml

Adjusting for reliability

Callers are frightened, and speech gets misheard, so no single statement is allowed to rule a room out.

A floor under every factor. A room that does not match a statement keeps 10% of its weight. One wrong clue can cost a room at most a factor of 10, and later evidence can win it back.

Confidence as an exponent. Each statement carries a confidence c between 0 and 1. A room type said outright counts in full; a smell counts half as much, so its worst case is a factor of 3.2, not 10.

Named rooms. "I'm in 302" moves room 302 to at most 95%, whatever its prior was; a hesitant "maybe 302, I'm not sure" gets about 50%. The reader's overall conclusion is applied last and capped at 90%. The dispatcher can reject any statement, which removes it entirely.

F_i(r) = [0.1 + 0.9 × m_i(r)]^c_i

planning/search_order.py · planning/exact.py

Search order: the most probability per unit of time

The most likely room is not always the right first stop. A slightly less likely room next to the entrance can be checked on the way. The planner minimises the expected time to find the caller: each stop's chance of being the one, times the time at which that stop is finished.

Small problems are solved exactly. Up to 12 candidate rooms, a dynamic programme finds the best order in about 3 ms. Above that, rooms are picked greedily by probability per second of travel and search, then improved by swapping and moving stops. The result is never worse than nearest-first or highest-probability-first, and the console shows all three times.

A look can miss. A rapid look finds a person who is there 90% of the time; a thorough look 97%, at twice the time. A searched room keeps what is left of its probability and can come back as a second look. After each unsuccessful look the whole picture is updated and the plan rebuilt from where the crew stands.

E[T] = Σ p_find(k) × C_k / Σ p_find(k) p_find(k) = p_k × (1 − q)^(earlier looks) × q greedy index = p_find(k) / (travel + look time)

situation/survival.py · configs/situations.yaml

Survival drop-off, and how it changes the route

What a minute costs depends on the emergency, and so does whether the route should change.

Witnessed, bystander CPR Witnessed, no CPR Not witnessed

Cardiac arrestSurvivalLost in the next minute

Witnessed: Valenzuela et al. 1997, assuming collapse 1.2 min before the call and the first shock 1 min after reaching the patient. Not witnessed: De Maio et al. 2003, fitted to about 10 minutes and extrapolated after.

Cardiac arrest bends the route. Survival falls on a curve: steeply at first, then flattening. A minute saved early is worth more than a minute saved late, so the planner stops minimising time and instead maximises the chance of reaching the patient while they can still be saved. In practice it pushes harder toward the likeliest rooms first and accepts a longer tail.

maximise Σ p_find(k) × S(arrival at k)

A steady loss leaves the route alone. For stroke, internal bleeding and fire the published figures are a constant loss per minute. When every minute costs the same, the fastest expected search is already the best one, so the order does not change. The console shows the cost so the dispatcher sees what a delay means.

EmergencyPoints lost per minute
Stroke (good outcome)0.08
Internal bleeding (mortality)0.35
Fire, victim not yet reached3.75

Conditions change the route too. With smoke reported, crews move at 0.5 m/s and search along the walls, so large rooms take far longer and lifts are never offered. For an active threat the console shows no route at all.

How well it reads callers

78 test calls written the way people talk: room numbers and tenant names, misheard words, corrections, yes and no answers, descriptions of the layout, and vague fragments that only place the caller when put together. Each call has a known true room. The reader is scored on where that room ends up in the console's ranking.

Probability on the true room at the end of the call: 0.81 with Claude, 0.54 with keyword rules. Calls with no usable location stay empty in 7 of 8 cases for both. Model time per caller turn: 3.2 s on average, 5.0 s at the 90th percentile.

An example of fragments adding up. A caller in the Julis Romo Rabinowitz Building (208 rooms, five levels) says, over three turns, "I'm up high, like one of the upper floors", "it was really far from where I came in" and "the name on the door starts with a B". None of those names a room. Together they leave the Bendheim Center for Finance on the top level, which the console ranks first.

Why this model. Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 were run on the same calls. Sonnet placed the caller as often (both 90% top room in the first round), was more often in the top three, answered about a second faster per turn and cost half as much, so it is the default. Raising its reasoning effort to medium changed the results by one call and added more than a second at the slow end, so it runs at low effort.

Where it still misses: one caller described the stairs they came in by, and the model read that as where they are. Results and the exact calls are in eval/results/; rerun with uv run python -m eval.run_agent_eval --modes rules,llm.

Console tour

The console is for the dispatcher. What you can do on your first call:

  1. Pick a buildingUse the building menu in the header. The synthetic 100 Princeton Tech Center needs no data or keys; real buildings appear once they're built.
  2. Step through the callPress Next Call Turn to add evidence one turn at a time and watch the heatmap and search plan change.
  3. Type what the caller saidUse the Caller said box for anything free-form. With an Anthropic key configured, Claude reads each turn against the building's layout and every record shows the caller's words it rests on; without one, the offline keyword rules read it. Reject any record you don't trust.
  4. Mark a room searchedMark Cleared on a stop means the crew searched it and didn't find the caller. That room's probability drops and the plan is rebuilt from where the crew is standing.
  5. Look inside and aroundOn real buildings, 3D opens the Matterport scan of a room and Approach shows aerial imagery with entrances and staging spots.
